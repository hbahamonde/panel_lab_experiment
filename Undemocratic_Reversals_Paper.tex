\begin{abstract}
Democratic backsliding often proceeds through lawful choices that weaken constraints on later decisions. Yet we know far more about why citizens surrender institutional safeguards than about whether they reclaim them once the crisis that justified concentrated authority recedes. We studied this reverse movement in an incentivized public-goods experiment with 300 participants in Finland. Citizens repeatedly decided whether an unknown leader's proposal required group approval or took effect automatically. Automatic implementation initially offered higher productivity and assured coordination, making weaker constraints materially attractive. We then randomly assigned matching pools to continued crisis or structural recovery, which made approval equally productive while leaving the leader's authority unchanged. The central outcome was whether citizens who had authorized decisions without approval subsequently voted to restore it. The design treated democratic reversal as a consequential choice over decision rights rather than a declaration of democratic sentiment. It therefore identified whether structural recovery caused citizens to withdraw the support that sustains executive aggrandizement and whether democratically authorized losses of decision rights remained reversible.
\end{abstract}

\section{Research Question and Contribution}

Democratic backsliding depends not only on leaders who weaken constraints but also on citizens who authorize those changes. One common form is \emph{executive aggrandizement}, in which formally lawful decisions gradually reduce constraints on one-person authority. This study examined its demand-side microfoundation by asking whether citizens who supported surrendering an approval right later voted to restore it after a crisis receded. We define that within-person change in institutional support as {\bf (\emph{un})democratic reversal}.

We build on the democratic backsliding and public-good games literatures. Contemporary backsliding often advances without ending elections because citizens may support elected officials or lawful rule changes that reduce the approval, oversight, or veto constraints governing later decisions \parencite{Bermeo2016}. The politically relevant step is therefore not necessarily an explicit vote against democracy. It can instead be a \emph{democratic} vote that makes subsequent authority \emph{less} constrained. Threat and policy benefits can increase tolerance for weaker constraints \parencites{HetheringtonSuhay2011}{Goodman2021}{0b3bf}. Laboratory evidence similarly shows that gridlock can increase support for executive special powers \parencite{FortezaMussioPereyra2024}.

This literature mainly explains movement toward less constrained authority. We followed the reverse movement by observing whether the same citizens voted to restore an approval right after its crisis-based benefit receded. That second choice turned reversal into a consequential decision over an institutional constraint rather than a stated evaluation of democracy.

Public-good games supply an economic reason for weakening approval. Voluntary provision is vulnerable to free riding, which means keeping one's points while others fund the shared service. This individual incentive can erode cooperation, whereas centralized enforcement can coordinate contributions \parencites{FehrGachter2000,FischbacherGachter2010,Hamman2011}. Experiments also show that voting over institutions changes their performance \parencites{DalBo2010,SutterHaignerKocher2010,MarkussenPuttermanTyran2014}. We joined these literatures by making citizen approval both institutionally meaningful and economically costly.

In each round, five citizens began with 20 points each and voted between two rules for managing a shared public-services fund. Under both rules, the software selected one citizen uniformly at random after the vote. Each citizen therefore had the same one-in-five chance of leading that round. The leader proposed how many points every citizen placed in the fund and whether any collected points moved to the leader's payoff. The game multiplied the remaining fund points and divided the group return equally.

Under the approval-constrained rule (\Dproc), at least three of the other four citizens had to approve the complete proposal. Rejection returned the group to voluntary individual allocations. Under automatic implementation (\Aproc), the same proposal took effect without that approval vote. Interaction occurred inside a stable \emph{matching pool} of 5, 10, or 15 citizens. Larger pools were reshuffled into temporary groups of five before each round. A five-person pool played together throughout. The institutional ballot therefore selected an approval rule, not a known leader or proposal.

Everyone began with a public-service crisis. Participants read that \enquote{\emph{Parliament has failed to pass a budget for eight months, healthcare waiting times have doubled, and planning for public services has stalled.}} During Block 1, each fund point created 1.50 group points under \Dproc{} and 2.50 under \Aproc{}. Direct implementation therefore had a one-point \emph{productivity premium} and guaranteed that the leader's proposal was implemented. After Block 1, each matching pool received {\bf institutional recovery} or {\bf institutional persistence}. Recovery combined improved public-service conditions with a rise in the \Dproc{} rate to 2.50. Persistence retained the crisis and the 1.50 rate. The \Aproc{} rate and decision rule remained fixed.

The payoffs made the choice consequential. {\bf The \emph{allocation of decision rights} connected it to democratic backsliding}. A citizen who voted for \Aproc{} supported surrendering an approval vote before knowing the leader or proposal. A later vote for \Dproc{} supported restoring that safeguard after the crisis-based productivity advantage disappeared. \emph{The experiment therefore observed whether citizens voted to take back a specific constraint on authority rather than inferring reversal from a survey declaration}.

\begin{plainlanguagebox}
Five citizens voted before seeing who would lead or what the leader would propose. Under \Dproc{}, three of the other four citizens had to approve the proposal. Rejection returned everyone to individual fund allocations. Under \Aproc{}, the proposal took effect directly. In Block 1, these methods used rates of 1.50 and 2.50, respectively.

After Block 1, recovery improved public-service conditions and raised \Dproc{} to 2.50. Persistence left the crisis and rate gap in place. Among previous \Aproc{} supporters, a later \Dproc{} vote supported restoring the approval right. Comparing recovery with persistence identified how much the structural recovery package changed that reversal.
\end{plainlanguagebox}

\begin{keyinsightbox}
A decision can be democratically authorized while weakening the democratic constraints that govern what happens next. This resembles contemporary backsliding, which often proceeds through elections and formally lawful institutional changes rather than an outright abolition of democracy \parencite{Bermeo2016}.

In the experiment, the first majority vote was democratic. A citizen who chose \Aproc{} voted to let an unknown leader's later allocation and transfer take effect without the other citizens' approval. A majority for \Aproc{} removed that constraint while leaving the institutional ballot itself intact. A later vote for \Dproc{} supported restoring the approval right that the citizen had previously voted to surrender.

\medskip
\textbf{What matters is that citizens used one democratic decision to weaken their control over a subsequent decision and could later vote to take that control back.}
\end{keyinsightbox}


\section{Theory}

\subsection{Crisis and the productivity premium}

A crisis can make action without approval attractive when ordinary procedures produce delay or failed coordination. The experiment gave this argument material consequences. In Block 1, proposals requiring approval (\Dproc) used a 1.50 fund rate, while proposals implemented directly (\Aproc) used 2.50. Under \Dproc{}, rejection also returned the group to voluntary contributions and possible free riding. Direct implementation therefore offered both a productivity premium and assured implementation of the leader's equal allocation \parencites{FehrGachter2000,FischbacherGachter2010,Hamman2011}.

The personal-transfer option made the institutional constraint consequential. The leader could propose moving up to 20 fund points to their own payoff under either procedure. Under \Dproc{}, the other citizens saw that transfer before implementation and could reject it. Under \Aproc{}, they observed it only after it had taken effect. The experiment therefore concerned approval over discretion, not the prevalence of corruption in Finland.

\paragraph{H1: Authorization under crisis.} During Block 1, better outcomes under direct implementation (\Aproc{}) are associated with greater subsequent support, while larger observed transfers are associated with lower support. These are descriptive expectations about experience within the crisis environment.

\subsection{Citizen authorization and democratic constraint}

The institutional ballot occurred before leader selection and before the proposal. A vote for \Aproc{} therefore supported a rule rather than a known person or action. That ballot supported relinquishing participation in approving the next binding decision, whereas a vote for \Dproc{} supported retaining that right. {\bf The design thus provided an abstract microfoundation of \emph{executive aggrandizement} in which a \emph{democratic} choice \emph{weakened} the constraint governing what followed} \parencites{Bermeo2016,FortezaMussioPereyra2024}.

\paragraph{H2: Approval and discretion.} Approval declines as proposed personal transfers increase. When leaders expect citizens to reject transfers despite the earnings from an approved proposal, leaders under \Dproc{} propose smaller transfers than those under direct implementation. These are behavioral expectations within endogenously chosen rules, not a separate randomized effect.

\subsection{Recovery and democratic reversal}

Recovery was assigned at the matching-pool level. Pool \(g\) contained 5, 10, or 15 participants. Five-person pools played together throughout, while larger pools were reshuffled into groups of five before each round. Three votes produced a majority within each group. The entire pool received the same Block-2 condition. We use \(T_g=1\) for recovery and \(T_g=0\) for persistence. Let \(m_{gb}\) denote the approval-constrained fund multiplier in pool \(g\) and block \(b\). The productivity premium for direct implementation is

\begin{equation}
\delta_{gb}=2.50-m_{gb}
\label{eq:productivity-premium}
\end{equation}

Everyone faced \(\delta_{g1}=2.50-1.50=1\) in Block 1. Recovery was implemented as one structural package. Participants learned that public-service conditions had improved, and \(m_{g2}\) rose to 2.50, which set \(\delta_{g2}=0\). Persistence reported that the crisis continued and kept \(m_{g2}=1.50\), so \(\delta_{g2}=1\). The leader-selection rule, proposal set, approval threshold, rejection-to-voluntary-allocation rule, and \Aproc{} rate remained fixed.

The recovery--persistence comparison identified the total causal effect of structural recovery on (un)democratic reversal. Among final-round Block-1 supporters of \Aproc{}, the analysis compared later \Dproc{} votes in recovery pools (\(T_g=1,\delta_{g2}=0\)) with those in persistence pools (\(T_g=0,\delta_{g2}=1\)). The public-service description stated what changed, and the production rate made that change materially consequential inside the game. Together, they defined the recovery package. The estimand therefore captured their combined effect rather than separate framing and payoff effects. Persistence supplied the counterfactual in which both the crisis and premium remained.



\paragraph{H3: Immediate restoration.} Among participants who vote for \Aproc{} in Block 1's final round, recovery increases the probability of voting for \Dproc{} in Block 2's first round.

\paragraph{H4: Short-run reversal.} Among the same participants, recovery increases the probability of voting for \Dproc{} in Block 2's fifth and final round, after four rounds of experience in the assigned environment.

\section{Experimental Design}

\subsection{Overview}

Each laboratory cohort completed one session of approximately 60 minutes with ten crisis rounds in Block 1 followed by five rounds in Block 2. The University of Turku issued a favorable ethical-review statement on 30 September 2026 (case TY/1722/2026).\footnote{Protocol amendments require the applicable ethics review before implementation.} Sessions took place at the UTU Choice Lab decision-making laboratory, which has 24 regular seats and one accessible seat. Production rosters contained 5, 10, 15, or 20 participants. Five-person sessions were an attendance fallback. Larger sessions retained anonymous rematching within stable ten- or fifteen-person pools. After Block 1, each entire pool was assigned to recovery or persistence. The pool then learned whether public-service conditions had improved or remained strained and saw its Block-2 fund rates. In the figures, \(p_i\) identifies participant \(i\).

\subsection{The institutional-choice public-good game}

Each citizen started with 20 points and privately voted between an approval-constrained rule (\Dproc) and automatic implementation (\Aproc). Let \(V_{i,b,r}\in\{\Dproc,\Aproc\}\) denote citizen \(i\)'s ballot in block \(b\) and round \(r\). Three votes selected the binding rule. The software then selected each group's leader through an independent uniform draw among its five citizens. Selection occurred under both rules and only after the institutional ballot. Every citizen therefore had the same chance before the draw in every round, and past service did not predict the next draw.

\paragraph{Leader proposal.} Under either rule, the leader proposed an equal allocation $t\in\{0,\ldots,20\}$ and a personal transfer $\rho\in\{0,\ldots,20\}$, with $\rho\leq5t$. Let $\pi$ denote a citizen's payoff in that round. Under \Aproc{}, the proposal took effect directly, and a nonleader earned
\begin{equation}
\pi^{A}_{igbr}=20-t+\frac{2.50}{5}(5t-\rho),
\label{eq:automatic-nonleader}
\end{equation}
and the leader earned $\pi^{A,L}_{igbr}=\pi^{A}_{igbr}+\rho$.

\paragraph{Approval-constrained procedure (\Dproc).} The four nonleaders observed $(t,\rho)$ and voted privately. Let $q_{igbr}=1$ denote approval. The proposal passed when at least three approved. If it passed, the payoffs above applied with $m_{gb}$ replacing 2.50. If it failed, each citizen chose $c_{igbr}\in\{0,\ldots,20\}$ and earned
\begin{equation}
\pi^{D}_{igbr}=20-c_{igbr}+\frac{m_{gb}}{5}\sum_{j=1}^{5}c_{jgbr}.
\label{eq:approval-fallback}
\end{equation}
The rejected transfer was not implemented. Everyone then saw the proposal, approval total, implemented allocation, transfer, and own payoff.

\begin{plainlanguagebox}
Rejection restored the standard \textbf{public-goods dilemma}. If one citizen contributed 1 point and four contributed 0, the contributor earned $19+\frac{1.50}{5}=19.30$ during the crisis, while each free-rider earned $20.30$. Under recovery, these payoffs became $19.50$ and $20.50$. Contributing helped the group but cost the contributor. Direct implementation avoided that risk, whereas approval allowed citizens to block an unwanted proposal.
\end{plainlanguagebox}

\paragraph{How incentives map onto reversal.} During Block 1, \Aproc{} combined a 2.50 rate with guaranteed implementation. By contrast, \Dproc{} used 1.50 and could return to voluntary contributions after rejection. Recovery reported improved public-service conditions and raised \Dproc{} to 2.50, while persistence retained the crisis and rate gap. The proposal set, approval rule, rejection procedure, and \Aproc{} rule remained unchanged. The recovery--persistence contrast therefore estimated the total effect of this structural package.

\paragraph{From institutional choice to (un)democratic reversal.} A citizen who moved from \Aproc{} to \Dproc{} first voted to let an unknown leader's proposal take effect without approval and later voted to restore that approval. The public-good incentives made this act consequential. After recovery, \Aproc{} still guaranteed implementation of the leader's equal allocation, whereas \Dproc{} could produce rejection and free riding. A higher switching rate under recovery would therefore indicate that structural change caused citizens to withdraw support for a weakly constrained decision rule. \emph{Reversal was observed as a vote to restore a decision right rather than as a declaration of democratic sentiment.}

Recovery made an approved \Dproc{} proposal equally productive, but it did not make that proposal pay more. \Aproc{} still guaranteed implementation. {\bf The question was whether citizens voted to restore approval once it became equally productive.}
\end{payoffbox}

\paragraph{Monetary benchmark.} With own earnings as the sole objective, a leader maximizes the round payoff at $t=20$ and $\rho=20$. If citizens approve whenever acceptance pays more than rejection followed by free riding, this proposal passes under \Dproc{}. During the crisis, it pays the leader 44 and each nonleader 24, compared with 20 each after rejection. Under \Aproc{}, it pays 60 and 40, respectively. Recovery makes the approved \Dproc{} proposal identical to \Aproc{}, so both give an expected payoff of 44 before the random leader draw. The benchmark therefore favors \Aproc{} during the crisis and leaves the institutional vote tied after recovery. Lower transfers and rejected profitable proposals require behavioral explanations. Because approval requires three votes, nonpivotal ballots can also support weak voting equilibria. This calculation is a payoff-responsive benchmark rather than a unique equilibrium prediction.

Participants reported expected payoffs and transfers before the final Block-1 ballot and the first and final Block-2 ballots. Expected payoffs assessed whether they understood the rules' relative performance. Expected transfers traced beliefs about how leaders would use their discretion.


\begin{plainlanguagebox}
Five citizens cast private institutional ballots ($V_{i,b,r}$), after which the software selected a leader. That leader proposed $(t,\rho)$. Under \Dproc{}, three of four nonleaders had to approve. Rejection activated individual contributions $(c_{igbr})$. Under \Aproc{}, the proposal took effect directly. Everyone saw the result and payoff before the next round. Only larger pools were rematched.
\end{plainlanguagebox}

\subsection{Block 1: crisis and leader authorization}

After consent, participants practiced proposing, approving, rejecting, and making individual allocations after rejection. They then answered five comprehension questions. The interface never called either method democratic or undemocratic.

Block 1 began with the public-service crisis described above. Each fund point produced 1.50 under \Dproc{} and 2.50 under \Aproc{}. The leader could propose the same allocation and transfer under either rule. The rules differed in whether the other citizens could reject that proposal.

Each paid round repeated the same sequence. Citizens cast an institutional ballot, the leader made a proposal, and the proposal either faced approval or took effect directly. Rejection activated individual allocations before feedback and, in larger pools, rematching. We elicited expectations before the final ballot and selected one Block-1 round for payment.

\subsection{Block 2: structural change and reversal}

After Block 1, pools were assigned to \emph{recovery} or \emph{persistence}. Recovery reported that the budget had passed, waiting times were falling, and planning had resumed. It also raised the \Dproc{} multiplier from 1.50 to 2.50. Persistence retained the crisis and the 1.50 rate. The \Aproc{} rate, leader selection, proposal set, approval threshold, and rejection procedure remained unchanged. Together, the updated description and rate formed the structural-recovery treatment.

The first Block-2 ballot measured immediate support for restoring approval. The fifth and final ballot measured reversal after four rounds of experience and served as the primary outcome. We elicited expectations before both ballots and asked about democratic constraints only after paid decisions. One Block-2 round was selected for payment.


\section{Outcomes, Process Measures, and Estimands}

An \emph{estimand} is the population-level quantity that an analysis seeks to recover. We first define the individual choices that compose the primary estimand.

Let \(\ind{\cdot}\) equal 1 when the condition inside braces is true and 0 otherwise. The final Block-1 ballot defines endorsement of automatic implementation as
\begin{equation}
A_{i1}=\ind{V_{i,1,10}=\Aproc}.
\end{equation}
For the theoretically relevant subgroup with $A_{i1}=1$, reversal is
\begin{equation}
R_i=\ind{V_{i,2,5}=\Dproc}.
\end{equation}
{\bf The primary estimand is the average treatment effect of structural recovery among Block-1 supporters of automatic implementation}:
\begin{equation}
\tau_R=\mathbb{E}[R_i(1)-R_i(0)\mid A_{i1}=1],
\end{equation}
where \(R_i(1)\) and \(R_i(0)\) are participant \(i\)'s potential outcomes under recovery and persistence. Recovery includes both improved public-service conditions and the 2.50 \Dproc{} rate, so \(\tau_R\) is the total effect of that package. Because $A_{i1}$ was measured before assignment, subgroup membership was fixed before treatment. The primary randomization test evaluated the sharp null of no recovery effect for any eligible citizen by reassigning treatment according to the prespecified pool-level procedure. Because \(\tau_R\) describes the average eligible citizen, the primary estimate weighted eligible participants equally. A secondary analysis gave each pool equal weight. Regression estimates clustered uncertainty by pool and included prespecified indicators for five- and fifteen-person pools. Missing final ballots were reported by condition, with sensitivity checks retaining all randomized eligible citizens in the accounting.

Immediate reversal is
\begin{equation}
R_i^{I}=\ind{V_{i,2,1}=\Dproc}\quad\text{for }A_{i1}=1.
\end{equation}
The same comparison applied to \(R_i^{I}\) estimated immediate support for restoration. The final-round outcome \(R_i\) remained primary because it recorded support after further interaction. Choosing \Dproc{} at both endpoints indicated short-run persistence, without requiring an uninterrupted sequence of \Dproc{} votes.

\begin{plainlanguagebox}
The primary estimand ($\tau_R$) asks how much recovery changed reversal among citizens who had voted for direct implementation in Block 1's final round ($V_{i,1,10}=\Aproc$, $A_{i1}=1$). Reversal means voting for group approval in Block 2's fifth and final round ($V_{i,2,5}=\Dproc$, $R_i=1$). Comparing recovery ($T_g=1$) with persistence ($T_g=0$) identified this difference. Only one potential outcome was observed per citizen. Assignment and inference operated at pool level.
\end{plainlanguagebox}

For each leader $i$, $t_{igbr}$ is the proposed allocation and $\rho_{igbr}$ the proposed transfer. A transfer proposal is $M_{igbr}=\ind{\rho_{igbr}>0}$, with intensity $\rho_{igbr}/(5t_{igbr})$ when $t_{igbr}>0$. Thus, $t=10$ and $\rho=5$ proposes moving 10\% of the 50 collected points. Approval ballots $q_{igbr}$, proposal passage, contributions after rejection, and subsequent institutional ballots provide process evidence. Comparisons between the endogenously chosen rules are descriptive and do not isolate the effect of requiring approval.

Immediate reversal \(R_i^{I}\) recorded the first response to the structural package before Block-2 interaction (see \autoref{fig:decision-timeline}). The primary outcome \(R_i\) recorded Round 5 after four completed rounds. Secondary outcomes included automatic-implementation support averaged over Rounds 3--5 and realized group-level restoration. These measures captured short-run adaptation rather than long-run convergence.

Expected payoffs assessed whether participants understood how recovery changed the rules' relative performance, while expected transfers traced beliefs about leader discretion. After all paid choices, eleven process items measured perceived crisis severity and recovery, expected institutional performance, and views on approval, equal allocation, and transfers. A separate optional twelve-item background module recorded age group, gender, education, household financial strain, party voted for in Finland's 2023 parliamentary election, left--right placement, whether democracy is preferable to other forms of government, satisfaction with democracy in Finland, evaluation of a strong leader unconstrained by parliament and elections, external political efficacy, generalized trust, and willingness to take risks. These prespecified measures captured partisan and ideological trade-offs, distinguished principled democratic support from evaluations of democratic performance, and benchmarked cooperative and risk-related predispositions \parencites{GRAHAM2020,0b3bf,Claassen2020,Dohmen2011,Glaeser2000}. We collected them only after paid decisions. They supported descriptive heterogeneity analyses but did not independently identify a treatment mechanism. Primary precision adjustment used only prespecified characteristics fixed before treatment and Block-1 measures, not potentially treatment-responsive attitudes collected afterward.

\section{Randomization, Sample, and Design Sensitivity}

The study targeted 300 completed participants. Larger laboratory cohorts were preferred, with five-person sessions available when attendance was insufficient for rematching. The realized pool mix determined the number of pool-level randomization units, ranging from 20 if all pools contained fifteen participants to 60 if all contained five. The maximum payment of EUR~15 set the compensation ceiling for 300 completions at EUR~4,500.

Treatment was assigned only after Block 1. Separately for each pool size, successive pools entered four-pool \emph{randomization sets} containing two recovery and two persistence assignments in random order (\autoref{fig:treatment-allocation}). These sets continued across laboratory sessions. The software stored each pool's set and position, concealed unused assignments, and reused the recorded allocation after an interruption. An incomplete final set retained its randomly drawn prefix. Balance therefore held within each completed set, rather than necessarily within a session or the final sample.

Compensation depended on one selected round per block, not the number of rounds played. Under the monetary benchmark above, each selected round yielded 44 expected points before leader selection, giving EUR~$10+88/24\approx13.67$ per participant and approximately EUR~4,100 for 300 completions. This was a conditional illustration rather than a spending forecast. The EUR~4,500 ceiling covered all completed-participant payments but left no allowance for additional recruitment or operating costs.

The number of pools determined the amount of independent treatment information. A larger pool supplied more individual outcomes but still received one treatment assignment. Across 300 completions, Block 1 generated 600 leader proposals and Block 2 generated 300. These repeated observations revealed trajectories, but did not create additional assignment units. Five-person pools added units per participant while exposing the same citizens to a shared group history, which could increase similarity within pools.

We evaluated design sensitivity with a simulation that reproduced pool-level treatment assignment, the eligible subgroup, the Round-5 outcome, and the two-sided randomization test at the five-percent level. \emph{Power} is the probability that this test detects a genuine recovery effect of an assumed size. It depends on the share eligible for the primary outcome, the reversal rate under persistence, and the \emph{intrapool correlation} (ICC), which measures how similar outcomes are within a pool. A 20-percentage-point effect means a reversal rate 0.20 higher under recovery. We varied pool composition and allowed greater correlation in fixed five-person groups. The simulations assumed independence between pools, not between their members.

The simulations supported five-person sessions as an attendance fallback rather than a guaranteed power gain. With 20\% of participants in five-person pools, power remained near 64\% for a 20-point effect and 81\% for 25 points, even allowing substantially greater fixed-group correlation. A mix including fifteen-person pools produced slightly lower power. Effects of approximately 25 percentage points reached 80\% power under these assumptions, while smaller effects remained less reliably detectable. In the ten-person reference design, a 20-point effect required approximately 440 completions, beyond the compensation budget. Further checks allowed lower eligibility and weaker recovery effects in fixed groups. Those changes reduced power, so the attendance mix and behavioral parameters remained planning uncertainties rather than assured gains.

The simulation conditioned on the treatment effect at the fifth ballot. Five Block-2 rounds supplied four rounds of experience before that choice, but did not establish behavioral convergence. In illustrative scenarios where each completed round closed 20\%, 50\%, or 80\% of the remaining distance from the first ballot to an assumed later limit, the fifth ballot captured 59\%, 94\%, or nearly 100\% of that additional adjustment. The first ballot could already reflect an immediate treatment response. These were sensitivity assumptions, not estimates from participants. Slower adjustment could produce a smaller Round-5 effect and lower power. The checks in \texttt{analysis/round\_horizon\_checks.R} kept this uncertainty separate from the exact monetary benchmark.

The primary randomization test reproduced the recorded size-specific randomization sets, including incomplete sets. Regression estimates clustered uncertainty by pool, adjusted for pool-size indicators, and used small-sample inference. Equal-pool weighting and, when estimable, an analysis excluding five-person pools checked sensitivity to the realized mix. Because attendance determined pool size, differences between fixed and rematched groups were descriptive rather than causal effects of matching.

Strategic decision screens showed a 90-second countdown and reminded participants to choose an answer and click Next. At zero, the reminder remained visible and the page stayed open. The software recorded response time and flagged delays without generating a ballot or allocation. The researcher or a trained research assistant monitored progress and helped delayed participants complete the current decision.

\section{Implementation, Ethics, and Project Timeline}

Adults registered for UTU Choice Lab through UTU RPP/TOKA, which managed recruitment and attendance separately from the experiment. Before practice, oTree presented study-specific information and recorded consent. Participants learned that the detailed research question would be explained after the paid task because advance disclosure could influence the institutional ballot. All task rules, payments, data processing, foreseeable risks, and the right to stop were disclosed beforehand. The debrief explained the full research purpose after the final questionnaires and any optional feedback.

The researcher or a trained research assistant fixed a consenting roster of 5, 10, 15, or 20 before paid play. They formed the matching pools in \autoref{tab:attendance-pools} and monitored synchronized progress. No participant entered or changed pools after paid play began. Technical testing verified fixed-group and rematching sessions, cross-session allocation, both conditions, wait pages, response-time reminders, browser recovery, exports, and payments. In the first ten-person session, an optional six-question pre-test assessed instruction clarity, the distinction between methods, payment clarity, screen difficulties, suggested improvements, and participants' understanding of the study's purpose. It followed all outcome and background questions and preceded debriefing. A separate form pooled feedback without study codes, response timestamps, or links to experimental or payment records. This feedback option was disabled for subsequent sessions. Any substantive protocol changes arising from the pre-test required review before further collection and explicit treatment in the analysis plan.

Live oTree data were hosted on University of Turku IT servers in Finland. The public interface did not collect research data, and oTree used no artificial-intelligence functions. UTU RPP/TOKA received no oTree codes, experimental responses, or payment information. The research team made immediate payments using a temporary code-and-amount list. Any named receipt contained no oTree code. The temporary list was destroyed after payment reconciliation and no later than 30 November 2026. Anonymization also required removing or coarsening identifying combinations and a documented disclosure-risk review. Until that review confirmed anonymization, coded data remained access-restricted to Janne Tukiainen, Hector Bahamonde, and authorized personnel.

\section{Payments and Laboratory Operations}

Participants received a EUR~10 show-up payment plus a points-based performance bonus capped at EUR~5. For 300 completed participants, total compensation could not exceed EUR~4,500. At completion, the interface displayed the selected Block-1 round, selected Block-2 round, point total, performance payment, participation payment, and total payment separately.

Both blocks ran during one synchronized session. The transition page was released only after every active participant had completed Block 1, so members of a matching pool encountered the new environment together. Participant codes rather than names linked decisions across blocks. All payments were made after Block 2.

The operational protocol fixed the completed roster before Block 1 and formed the pool configuration in \autoref{tab:attendance-pools}. No participant entered or changed pools after paid play began. Strategic screens showed a 90-second countdown followed by a reminder to choose and click Next, while remaining open. The software recorded response time and flagged delays without generating an answer. The researcher or trained research assistant monitored these flags and prompted delayed participants to complete the current decision. The session log documented late arrival, browser failure, early departure, manual intervention, and payment exceptions. Preregistered exclusion rules did not depend on treatment-consistent behavior.

Technical testing covered 5-, 10-, and 15-person pools, mixed-pool sessions, both treatment branches, wait pages, cross-session allocation, rematching, decision reminders, refresh persistence, and payment calculations. The first ten-person session also offered six optional usability questions after the main questionnaires and before debriefing. Feedback was pooled separately without codes or links to experimental records, then the feedback option was disabled. Substantive changes required protocol review and prespecified handling of the pre-test observations.

\section{Experience Across Repeated Play: Design Safeguards}

Repeated play revealed whether institutional choices persisted after participants experienced their consequences. The design therefore separated those consequential responses from avoidable procedural adaptation.

\paragraph{Procedural familiarity.} Participants could initially misunderstand payoff rules. Guided unpaid practice and mandatory comprehension checks established familiarity before paid decisions. Block-2 Round 1 recorded the immediate response, while Round 5 recorded short-run reversal and Rounds 3--5 summarized late behavior.

\paragraph{Reputation and reciprocity.} Anonymous rematching within ten- or fifteen-person pools limited stable-team effects. Five-person pools retained their membership and could develop stronger shared histories. Participants never received persistent identities. Pool-size adjustment and, when estimable, exclusion of five-person pools assessed sensitivity to this difference.

\paragraph{Leader-role expectations.} Leader selection occurred after the institutional ballot and used an independent uniform draw among the five citizens under both procedures. Each citizen therefore had a one-in-five chance in every round. Citizens could not vote knowing whether they would make the proposal or use prior service to predict the next leader.

\paragraph{Endogenous institutional experience.} Groups that never elected one procedure received less experience with it. Instructions and practice exposed everyone to both payoff rules, and we recorded realized experience.\footnote{We followed \textcite{Bigoni:2012} and implemented a short and effective set of instructions.} We reported treatment effects by prior exposure, but the primary randomized comparison did not condition on a post-treatment institutional path.

\paragraph{Wealth, fatigue, and end-game effects.} Only one randomly selected round per block determined game earnings, so earnings from early rounds did not alter later endowments. Payment chances were one in ten for each Block-1 round and one in five for each Block-2 round. The ten-plus-five-round horizon was disclosed and identical across conditions. Recorded decision times and delay flags identified fatigue or end-game behavior. Both conditions experienced the same fifteen-round sequence, which separated the treatment effect from generic within-session fatigue.

\paragraph{Cross-block comparability.} Labels, leader selection, proposal choices, approval threshold, fallback, endowments, and sequence remained fixed. Recovery jointly improved public-service conditions and raised the approval-constrained multiplier. Persistence identified change from repeated play or approaching the session's end.

These safeguards preserved experience with institutional consequences while limiting changes driven by the interface, accidental partners, or accumulated wealth.

\section{Ethics and Interpretation}

The experiment made action without approval materially effective but avoided moral labels. Instructions used ``Group approval required'' and ``Decision takes effect directly,'' disclosed every rule, and reserved explicit questions about democratic constraints until paid choices were complete.

Registration through UTU RPP/TOKA covered the laboratory's general rules and privacy notice. Before practice, the interface presented study-specific information on the session, interaction, anonymity, payment, data handling, and the importance of remaining for the full task. It then recorded affirmative consent to this study. The researcher or trained research assistant handled nonconsent before the group task began. The debrief explained the democratic interpretation only after all incentivized decisions and clarified that no single vote or transfer established that a participant was corrupt, authoritarian, or opposed to democracy. Data exports used coded study IDs. UTU RPP/TOKA did not receive oTree codes, experimental responses, or payment data. The research team used a separate temporary code-and-amount list for immediate payment and destroyed it after reconciliation.

The Finnish setting strengthened the case for a hypothetical, incentive-based task. Participants did not judge Finnish officials. They faced a fictional crisis and transparent, consequential rules. Recovery was the joint improvement in that environment and the approval-constrained production rate.

The interpretation centered on revealed choice over approval. Comparing recovery with persistence identified whether the structural package caused previous \Aproc{} supporters to restore that constraint. It did not separate the package's narrative and payoff components.

\section{Interpretive Decision Rule}

The principal conclusion followed a simple rule. Evidence for structural reversal required a positive, substantively meaningful, and statistically distinguishable recovery effect on $R_i$ among Block-1 \Aproc{} supporters. Approval, proposals, transfers, fallback contributions, and expectations described behavior around that effect.

The design isolated a microfoundation of executive aggrandizement. Citizens used a democratic ballot to remove approval from a later decision and could restore it after structural recovery. Democratic reversal was the final-round vote to reinstate that constraint after further interaction.
