Debriefing

During the task, the two methods were described as Each person chooses and One person chooses for the group. The study uses this contrast to examine a behavioral foundation of democratic backsliding: whether people authorize one-person control when it has a material advantage, and whether they later withdraw that authorization.

For some matching pools, public-service conditions improved and the fund rates became equal in Block 2. For other pools, the crisis continued and fund points remained more productive when one person chose for the group. In both cases, the selected person's rule was unchanged: that person could require the same amount from all five members and move fund points to their own payoff.

The study therefore examines whether participants return to individual choice after the productivity advantage of one-person control disappears, even though one decision-maker can still coordinate the group's allocations. No single choice is interpreted as evidence that a participant is corrupt, authoritarian, or opposed to democracy.

Changing a choice is not treated as inconsistency. The study examines how institutional choices respond when public-service conditions and their material consequences change.
